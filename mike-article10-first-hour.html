The First Hour:
Why the Start of Your Day Decides the Rest

The first hour is the only hour with no competition. Most of us give it away without noticing.

By
M.O.
Date
October 29, 2026

Think about the first thing you did this morning.

For a very large number of people, it was to reach for a phone and read a list of things other people want: messages, notifications, headlines, a calendar already filling up. It feels harmless, even responsible. You are simply getting ahead of things.

Book 1 of the GoBeyond Series, Energy, sees it differently. “Everything that follows is then a response. The day has a shape before you have chosen one, and it is somebody else’s shape.”

This article is about taking that shape back, and about why the first hour matters far more than its sixty minutes suggest.

The only hour with no competition

The book opens its chapter on the morning with a simple observation: “The first hour of the day is the only hour with no competition.”

That is not a claim that mornings are magical. It is a practical point. Every later hour has to be defended against requests, problems and other people’s priorities. The first hour does not, because nothing has arrived yet. Whatever you put there is the only thing there.

That makes it, in the book’s words, “the cheapest hour in the day to protect and the most expensive to give away.” Protecting it costs almost nothing. Giving it away costs the one moment in the day when you could choose what matters before anyone else told you.

What the hour is for

Energy suggests three things, taking somewhere between forty five and ninety minutes depending on how you arrange them.

Attention to something larger than the day. The author is plain about what this is for him: prayer and Scripture. In practical terms, it is unhurried, undemanding attention to something beyond your own agenda before the agenda arrives. The reason is the frame it sets. A frame chosen before the day’s demands is different from one assembled while you are already responding to them. If you don’t share that faith, the principle still holds: begin by attending to what is larger than today’s list.

Movement. Not necessarily training. Just enough physical engagement to signal to your body that the day has begun. Fifteen minutes will do. The point is less about fitness than about state: the shift from recovery to activity happens more cleanly when it is deliberate.

A decision about the day. Before anything has been asked of you, name the one to three things that must happen for this day to have counted. This is not a task list. It is a judgement, made from clarity rather than pressure, about what actually matters in the hours ahead. It takes about four minutes, and the book calls it “the difference between a day you directed and a day that happened to you.”

Why the order matters

The sequence is deliberate, though more flexible than any single prescription.

Attention comes first because it is the most easily displaced and the hardest to return to once the day is loud. Movement comes second because it works better on a settled mind than an agitated one. The decision comes last because it should be made from the state the first two produce, not the state you woke up in.

If you can only manage one of the three, the book is clear about which: “If you can only do one, do the third. Four minutes of deciding what matters, before the day tells you what matters, is worth more than most hours.”

The one rule

Everything rests on a single rule: nothing arrives until the hour is done.

No messages, no news, no feed, no calendar. Not because information is harmful, but because the moment you open the channel, the hour stops being yours. You can be interrupted at seven o’clock and recover. But you cannot be interrupted at seven and still have had an uninterrupted first hour, because what made it valuable was precisely that nothing had claimed it.

The book admits this is harder than it sounds, and easier than expected once the first fortnight is past. The real difficulty is rarely discipline. It is the belief that something urgent is waiting. That belief is easy to test: for a month, keep a note of anything that genuinely could not have waited ninety minutes. Most people who try this find the list close to empty.

When an hour is impossible

For some people, a protected hour each morning simply does not exist. A parent with a small child. Someone working nights. Someone whose body is not cooperating at the moment. Someone sharing a home that wakes all at once. The usual advice, get up earlier, is not always available or wise.

Energy takes this seriously, and makes two points.

First, “the hour is a container, not a clock.” What matters is a protected space before the day’s demands are admitted. For some, that is twenty minutes. For others it is ten o’clock the night before, preparing for the day that follows, or it is split into two short pieces. The principle survives each of these adaptations: the day gets a shape before it is given one.

Second, the short version is not failure. Four minutes of attention, four of movement and four of deciding comes to twelve minutes, and it keeps most of what matters. It is the presence of the practice that does the work, not its length. As the book puts it: “A twelve minute version maintained for a year beats a ninety minute version abandoned in March, and it is not close.”

What it actually gives you

It is worth being honest about what the first hour does not do. It does not create time. “It costs time, and anyone selling it as a productivity technique is selling it wrongly.”

What it gives you is a day entered rather than received. A day that begins in response mode tends to stay there, and decisions made in that state have a recognisable character: quicker, more defensive, shorter in horizon. Most people recognise it in hindsight and rarely notice it at the time.

It also changes what survives the day. The thing you named at seven has a much better chance of still standing at four than the thing you never named at all. And over months, it builds something harder to describe: the accumulated experience of having directed your own life rather than simply answered it.

One thing to try tomorrow

The book closes the chapter with three short prompts, and they are a good place to start.

Reflect. What is currently the first thing you do after waking? What does it set in motion?

Decide. How long your version will be. Twelve minutes and ninety minutes are both real answers.

Do it once. Tomorrow, open nothing until all three parts are done: attention, movement, a decision. Then judge it.

“The person who owns their first hour owns their day.” It is a small hour. It may be the most valuable one you have.
